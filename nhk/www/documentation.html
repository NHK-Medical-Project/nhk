NHK Medical — the whole system, in plain words

How our system works

This page explains the computer system NHK Medical runs on: what it does, what it is made of, and what is wrong with it. No technical knowledge needed. It takes about ten minutes to read.

1. What the company does

NHK Medical sells and rents breathing machines. They are called CPAP machines. People who stop breathing properly while they sleep use one at night to help them breathe.

The company does two things with these machines:

  • Sells them. You buy the machine and keep it forever.
  • Rents them. You borrow the machine for a while, pay for that time, and then NHK comes and takes it back.

The important bit: these machines do not get posted in a box. A person from NHK — a technician — drives to the patient's house, sets the machine up, comes back to fix it if it breaks, and comes back again at the end to collect it. So a big part of the system is about people driving around, not just about orders on a screen.

Money is collected by sending a payment link to the patient's phone. Almost all the messages to patients go out on WhatsApp.

2. What happens when someone buys a machine

  1. The patient ordersOn the website, or by telephoning the office.
  2. Staff say yesSomeone checks the order and approves it. The patient gets a message.
  3. The patient paysAll of it, or just part of it. Paying part is normal here.
  4. A technician delivers itDrives out, sets the machine up in the house.
  5. DoneThe order is finished and the bill is made.

3. What happens when someone rents a machine

Renting is the harder half, because the machine has to come back.

  1. The patient asks to rentThey say which dates they want it for.
  2. Staff say yesApproved before any machine is promised.
  3. One exact machine is pickedNot "a machine" — that machine, with its own serial number, like a bike's frame number.
  4. A technician delivers itDrives out and sets it up.
  5. The rental runsThe patient can keep it longer (a renewal), or be late paying (overdue).
  6. A technician collects itTakes it away and brings it back to the office.
  7. It gets checked and cleanedFixed if needed, then it can go out to the next patient.

Every single machine is tracked by its own number for its whole life — sold or rented, delivered, repaired, collected, rented again. The system keeps a permanent repair diary for each one.

4. Who uses the system

  • PatientsBrowse machines, order, pay, and check where their order is.
  • Sales staffTake orders, approve them, pick machines, chase people who have not paid.
  • Office adminRun the day, set the prices, fix mistakes, close rentals.
  • TechniciansDrive out. Deliver, set up, repair, collect. Write down every trip they make.
  • AccountsBills, payments, and the tax paperwork the government wants.
  • HRStaff records, leave, salaries.
  • Hospitals and doctorsThey send patients to NHK, and get credit for it.
  • Repair shopsOutside companies that fix broken machines.

5. What the system is built from

NHK did not write this whole system from nothing. They started with a ready-made one called ERPNext. It is free, and lots of companies use it. It already knows about orders, bills, stock and staff.

Think of it like this. ERPNext is a ready-made shop kit in a big box. You get the till, the shelves and the order book. Then each company adds their own bits on top for whatever is special about them.

There are six pieces of software running together here. Two of them were written specially for NHK:

  • frappeThe engine everything else sits on.
  • erpnextThe ready-made shop: orders, bills, stock.
  • nhkWritten for NHK. Rentals, technicians, payment links, the website.
  • webtoolex_whatsappWritten for NHK. Sends the WhatsApp messages.
  • india_complianceIndian tax rules and government paperwork.
  • hrmsStaff, leave and salaries.

6. The one big problem

This is the most important thing on this page. If you remember one thing, remember this one.

ERPNext is designed to have extra bits added to it. It has proper places to plug your own things in, so that your bits stay yours and ERPNext stays ERPNext.

NHK's extra bits were not plugged in. Instead, someone opened up ERPNext itself and wrote NHK's code inside it, mixed in with ERPNext's own code.

Think of it like this. LEGO has bumps on top so new bricks click on and come off again. Imagine instead melting your new bricks onto the old ones with a hot glue gun. It works! It looks fine! But now the two are one lump, and you can never swap either one out again.

That causes three real problems, and they are getting worse, not better:

  • Updates hurt. The people who make ERPNext release a better version every so often. Normally you just take it. Here, the new version fights with NHK's melted-in code every single time, and someone has to untangle it by hand.
  • Nobody can find anything. If a new programmer asks "what happens when an order is saved?", the normal way to look it up gives no answer. You have to read through a file six thousand lines long and hope you spot it.
  • Nothing starts without everything. ERPNext now refuses to even switch on unless NHK's own two pieces are there — but nowhere does it say so. Install them in the wrong order and it simply breaks.
The good news

This can be undone slowly, a bit at a time. Nobody has to fix it all in one go. Every piece that gets moved out of ERPNext and into NHK's own software makes the next update easier. This is the single most useful job on the list.

7. The other things that need fixing

These were all found by reading the code. None of them is on fire today. All of them will cost money or time if they are left alone.

  • Customers' passwords are saved as plain words worst one When someone signs up on the website, their password is stored in a way anyone in the office can read, like a list written on a whiteboard. It should be scrambled so nobody can read it — and the system already has a proper scrambled box built in. It just is not being used.
  • Nobody can see who changed what high Normally a program keeps a diary of every change ever made, so you can ask "who changed this, and when?" Here the diary has only one page: today. Everything before that was thrown away.
  • The card details for taking payments are also stored as plain words medium Same problem as the passwords, in a different place. These are the keys to the payment company, sitting in a settings page anyone in the office can open.
  • An order can be in 28 different states medium Twenty-eight labels for where an order has got to — and four of them look like they all just mean "finished". So two people looking at the same order pick different labels, and then the reports are wrong. Somebody needs to sit with the office and cross out the ones nobody uses.
  • The same order is written down three times medium There are three different kinds of order form for what is basically one thing. Two of them are almost exactly the same — 178 boxes to fill in, and only 2 are different. So every change has to be made three times, and anyone counting orders has to add up three separate piles.
  • A repeated piece of code, written twice medium One instruction was accidentally written out twice in the same file. Only the second one actually runs. Somebody fixing a bug could easily spend a day fixing the copy that never runs.
  • Emails are sent the risky way medium The system emails Gmail directly instead of using its own built-in postbox. So if an email fails, it is not tried again, and there is no record it was ever sent.
  • No rules written down for patient information medium The system holds names, addresses, phone numbers and photos of ID documents. Nobody has written down how long to keep them, who may download them, or who is allowed to look.
  • Some buttons exist nowhere in the code check this A few buttons that staff press every day were typed straight into the live system instead of being saved with the rest of the code. If that system were ever rebuilt from scratch, those buttons would simply be gone, and nobody would know why.

8. Words we all agreed to use

When ten people call the same thing five different names, everyone gets confused. So the project has an agreed list of words. These are the important ones:

  • DeviceOne actual physical machine, with its own number. Not "unit", not "equipment".
  • Sales OrderAn agreement to give someone a machine. Not "booking", not "SO".
  • Technician Visit EntryOne trip, to one house, for one reason. It is how technicians get paid, so it matters that it means exactly one thing. Not "job", not "call-out".
  • PickupGoing and collecting a rented machine back at the end. Not "return".
  • Payment LinkThe one-time link texted to a patient so they can pay.
  • IncentiveThe money a technician earns for doing a visit. This is not the same as what the visit cost the company — keeping those two apart matters.
Six words still have two meanings

The worst is "NHK User", which means two completely unrelated things: a customer who signed up on the website, and a permission badge given to office sales staff. Until somebody decides which is which, that phrase should not be used at all. There is also "Customer" and "Patient", which turn out to be the same person, written two ways.

9. Questions only the business can answer

Nobody can look these up in the code. Someone who actually runs NHK has to say.

  1. Which of those 28 order labels do you really use? Can we delete the rest?
  2. If a WhatsApp message fails to send, should the order stop — or should we just make a note of it?
  3. How long must we keep a patient's records, ID photos and signed forms?
  4. Who is allowed to download the customer list?
  5. How are technicians' payment rates decided, and how often do they change?
  6. When a rented machine comes back, what must happen before it can go out again?
  7. How quickly do we want to take new versions of ERPNext? That decides how urgent the big problem is.

10. One honest warning

This was all worked out backwards

Normally you write down what a system should do, and then build it. Here the system was built first, and all of this was worked out afterwards by reading the code that is already running.

So it describes what the system does, and treats that as the rule. If the code does something by mistake, this may have written the mistake down as if it were on purpose. Somebody who runs the business still needs to read it through and say "yes, that's right" — or "no, that's a bug".

All of this is written down in full in the bench notes: CONTEXT.md for the agreed words, docs/requirements.md for what the system must do, docs/apps.md for the six pieces of software, docs/core-modifications.md for the melted-in code, and docs/adr/ for the big decisions and why they were made.
